Dealing With Toilet Overflow Cleanup in Sherman

A toilet overflow looks like a mop job, but the water does not stay on the tile. Some slips beneath the baseboard and into the wall, and some drops through the opening around the drain pipe. The contents of that water matter more than anything. Clean water is what comes out of the supply line or the tank. Water that came up through the bowl is not, and it has to be handled as contaminated.

Our Sherman crew starts by identifying the water category, because that sets the rules for everything after. If it is clean, we extract it and dry the area. For gray or black water we wear protective gear, remove soaked porous items such as carpet pad, and disinfect each surface the water reached. Then we check the subfloor, the wall bases and the room underneath with moisture meters so nothing is left wet.

First Five Minutes

Before We Arrive

  1. Stop the WaterBehind the toilet is a small valve, and turning it fully clockwise shuts the water off. If it will not turn, lift the tank lid and raise the float to stop the fill.
  2. Do Not Flush AgainNo more flushing, and no running the washer, a shower or a sink. If the drain line is blocked, any water you send down can come back up.
  3. Stay Out of Contaminated WaterBowl water, or any water with waste in it, means kids and pets stay out of the room. Should you have to cross it, wear gloves and waterproof footwear.
  4. Look at the Ceiling UnderneathFor an upstairs toilet, look at the ceiling underneath. A ceiling that sags or drips means turning off that room's power and moving your things clear.
  5. Block the SpreadOld towels can stop clean water before it gets to a hallway or carpet. Anything contaminated is a job for people with proper gear and disinfecting products.

Will Insurance Pay?

Insurance and Toilet Overflow Cleanup

A sudden and accidental overflow, for example from an unexpected clog or a fill valve that failed, is covered by many homeowners policies. Sewer or drain backup is often excluded without a backup endorsement on the policy, and an overflow from a blocked main line can fall under that exclusion. Months of leaking around the base of a toilet usually falls outside coverage. To learn which of these applies in your case, check the policy or phone your agent.

Why It Happens

Why This Happens

  • Blocked traps and drain lines, which leave the bowl nowhere to empty but onto the floor.
  • Septic systems that are full or drain fields that are waterlogged, which send water back toward the house.
  • Tree roots or a collapsed section in the sewer line that slows every drain in the house.
  • A stuck flapper or fill valve that keeps the tank running without stopping.
  • Failed wax rings under the toilet, which leak at the floor on each flush.

Where the Damage Spreads

Vanity and Drywall Wicking

Water spreads to the vanity toe kick and the lower edge of the drywall, where it wicks upward. Several inches of wall above floor level can hold moisture with no visible sign on the paint.

Bacteria in Building Materials

Bowl and drain water leaves bacteria in carpet, pad, baseboards and drywall. Porous materials cannot be reliably disinfected and usually have to be removed.

Swollen Baseboards and Trim

The bottom edge of a baseboard or door casing draws in water as soon as the floor floods. Most are made of fiberboard, which swells, splits and does not go back to its original shape.

Downstairs Ceiling Damage

From a second floor bathroom, water follows the drain pipe down and pools on the ceiling drywall beneath, leaving stains, sagging and sometimes a collapse.
